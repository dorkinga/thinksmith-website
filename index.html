The order is the insight — and teaching others is where it locks in. STAGE 1

Challenge first

Before any lesson, your child writes their own solution to a real-life challenge, in their own words — not multiple choice, not fill-in-the-blank. They are not expected to get it right. The attempt creates the foothold — and the questions — that the new knowledge will answer.

No lesson first — this is the step other models leave to chance
STAGE 2

Learn what you need

Now the new knowledge arrives — often as a link to a free Khan Academy tutorial — at the moment your child needs it to improve their solution, so it lands on a question they're already asking, not on a blank slate. The pilot study called this exactly what it is: foundational knowledge acquired “on a need-to-know basis.” Ms. Rivera, their 24/7 personal AI coach, is beside them throughout: guiding their thinking, never supplying the answer.

STAGE 3

Teach one peer — the key step

Your child takes the teacher's role, reading three AI peers' attempts at the same challenge, then selecting one and writing constructive feedback to that peer, coaching them through the gap. There is no weakest link and no free ride — the peers are always prepared, at genuinely varying quality, and judging their reasoning forces your child to rethink their own.

This is the step most platforms skip entirely
STAGE 4

Reflect

They receive feedback on their own work and write a reflection on what changed in their thinking — the closest thing to a one-on-one teacher conference at this scale.

STAGE 5

See their Thinking Outcome

A synthesis across five competencies and a narrative of how their thinking developed that activity — and a clear read on whether they're ready to move on. They keep going if they want — the next challenge is theirs to choose.

Why the order matters

Same knowledge. Different order. Different result.

This is not a preference — it's how learning works. As the pilot study behind ThinkSmith puts it, following Duffy & Cunningham: learning is “an active process of constructing knowledge rather than acquiring knowledge.” Knowledge handed over first has nothing to be constructed with. The ThinkSmith order fixes that, four ways:

01

An attempt gives knowledge a place to land

Before any lesson, your child commits to their own idea — even a wrong one. New knowledge then has something real to connect to, build on, or correct. Without that foothold, knowledge is simply heard and forgotten: there is nothing in the learner's mind for it to attach to.

Students retain less than 20% of a lecture a week later — Guskin, cited in the study
02

A gap you discover yourself creates the need to know

Trying first shows learners what they don't know — to themselves. So when the knowledge arrives, it answers a question they're already asking, and they use it immediately in their own solution. Used knowledge is understood and remembered; knowledge memorised in isolation, for a test that hasn't happened yet, mostly isn't.

Foundational knowledge, acquired “on a need-to-know basis” — the pilot study
03

Feedback needs real thinking to work on

A coach, a peer, or a teacher cannot guide thinking that hasn't happened yet — all they can do is repeat the lesson. An attempt, and three peers' attempts, give feedback something specific to work on: the actual misunderstanding, revealed. In the pilot, class time stopped being a lecture and became that feedback — because the engagement had already happened.

100% of surveyed instructors: engage first, class time for feedback, was more productive
04

Teaching turns recall into judgement

Reviewing three peers' solutions — and coaching one through a gap — forces your child to compare, analyse, and evaluate — higher-order thinking from the very start, not as a reward after memorising the fundamentals. The pilot study notes this reversal of the traditional fixed order also helps learners keep the foundational knowledge, because they acquired it while using it. And learners take ownership: they build their own learning strategies instead of waiting to be taught.

92.9% of surveyed students took more responsibility for their own learning

Put together, that's the result the pilot measured: not better content, not better teachers — a better order, and final exam averages 17.31% higher across the ten courses that could be compared directly.

Our story

Built by someone who had to figure out how learning actually works.

ThinkSmith didn’t start as a business plan. It started with a student who had to work twice as hard to succeed in traditional classrooms that are designed for memorization and rote memorization, not deep understanding — and who later helped prove a better way across 1,742 university students.

Leslie Richards
Founder & Creator of ThinkSmith

I left school at 16 because traditional classrooms were designed for memorization, not deep understanding.

Growing up with dyslexia, reading, writing, and spelling didn’t come automatically. While other students seemed to breeze through lessons, I had to work relentlessly just to keep up. For decades, I relied on sheer persistence—and later, the arrival of the word processor—to bridge the gap between what I knew in my head and what I could put on paper.

That struggle gave me an advantage: it forced me to pay close attention to why certain explanations stick and why others bounce right off.

Years later, I led a semester-long pilot study with 1,742 university students across 22 STEM and engineering courses. The results changed everything. We proved that when learners engage with a real-world challenge before receiving a formal lesson, their comprehension and confidence skyrocket.

(Later awarded an honorary doctorate for research connected to the ThinkSmith approach.)

Most children don’t struggle because they lack ability. They struggle because traditional education dumps brand-new information on them before they have a mental foothold to catch it—and endless repetition doesn’t fix that.

Homeschooling already gives your child the freedom to learn at their own pace. ThinkSmith gives them the structure to think deeply, build real mastery, and thrive.

— Leslie Richards, Founder & Creator of ThinkSmith

Safeguarding

What we share if a child may be at risk

ThinkSmith is an educational tool, not a crisis line or counselling service. If a child is in immediate danger, please call your local emergency services.
How our safeguards work

ThinkSmith runs on two layers of protection: the foundation provided by the AI model itself, and the layer we build on top for our own classroom.

Tier 1 — Foundation. Our AI coach is built on Claude, made by Anthropic. Anthropic's own safety systems apply to everything the coach produces, preventing harmful, illegal, or exploitative content at the source. This protection is always on and does not depend on us.

Tier 2 — ThinkSmith. The foundation layer cannot know what a good lesson looks like, or what Ms. Rivera is supposed to be. That is our responsibility, and it is where we concentrate our work.

  • Every message a child writes is screened for signs of distress before our coach replies. If content indicates self-harm or serious distress, the coach does not answer the question — see below.
  • Every response we generate is screened before a child sees it. Screening what a child writes is not enough on its own — a perfectly innocent question can still produce an answer we would not want shown. So the coach's replies, and the work of the AI classmates, are checked independently before they appear. Anything flagged is discarded rather than displayed, and recorded for our team to review.
  • Ms. Rivera and the AI classmates are clearly identified as AI. Ms. Rivera is an AI coach, not a real person, and the classmates are not real children. This is stated on screen and marked beside every name, so a child is never left to work it out.
  • A child can tell you something is wrong, in one tap. Beside Ms. Rivera and beside each classmate's work there is a link to report it to you. They don't have to explain why or write anything, and nothing is locked or taken away because they used it. You are emailed straight away with exactly what they were looking at — Ms. Rivera's words, or the classmate's — and the child is shown plainly that you will see it.
  • Putting it right is our job, not yours. Our team is told at the same moment you are. You can't correct Ms. Rivera and we don't think you should have to try — if she said something she shouldn't have, we fix it, and someone gets in touch to tell you what we found.
  • Ms. Rivera has no memory of your child. She sees the challenge in front of her and, at most, the last few exchanges about that one piece of work. Nothing carries between challenges. She builds no profile, forms no impression over time, and cannot be gradually shaped by one child into something she is not. When a child submits the same work again, she reviews it fresh rather than judging them for asking twice.
  • She works to fixed teaching rules. She guides thinking rather than supplying answers — that is the whole point of ThinkSmith — and those rules are the same for every child, every time.
  • Every exchange is stored, and our team reviews them. Conversations between a child and the coach are retained and read by ThinkSmith team members as part of our ongoing quality and safety checks.

We are continuing to add to this layer. It is the part of the system we work on most.

If a child may be at risk
  1. Immediate support. The coach stops coaching and directs the child to a crisis service staffed by trained people — the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline in the United States, or Kids Help Phone in Canada.
  2. Human review. The alert is escalated to a ThinkSmith team member who has completed child-protection orientation.
  3. Reporting. Where a report to child-protection authorities is required by law, we notify the appropriate agency.
What information is shared

To allow an authority to assess immediate risk and contact a parent, we share only:

  • The learner's full name and grade
  • Their province or state
  • The parent or guardian's name and email address

We do not include a home address, date of birth, telephone number, location data or billing details in a safety report.

Household disclosures

If a child discloses a concern involving someone in their own household, we report to the authority without contacting the family first. A system that routed every disclosure straight back to a parent account could place a child at greater risk.

The research · parent version

How a 1,742-student university pilot proved that active thinking beats passive listening

When parents look for a learning approach for their children, they usually ask two questions: will this actually help my child learn better, and will it build the self-reliance they need for the future?

In 2008–2009, ThinkSmith founder Leslie Richards led a university pilot study involving 1,742 students across 22 courses in STEM, computer science and engineering at Ubon Ratchathani University.

The goal was simple: engage students in new knowledge before the lecture. Instead of sitting through passive hours of instruction, students used the ThinkSmith framework — active problem-solving, reviewing peer ideas, and receiving structured feedback ahead of the deep-dive discussion.

The findings parents care about
17.3%
higher final exam scores, across the 1,256 students where a direct comparison against traditional instruction was possible
44.8%
final exam improvement in CNC Computer-Aided Design & Manufacturing — failures and withdrawals fell from 10 to 3
27.6%
higher final exam average in Calculus III, a retake course with a history of frequent failure
Students gained confidence and took ownership

Academic improvement only happens when a learner’s mindset changes first. In a post-study survey of 684 students:

  • 93.9% found ThinkSmith more rewarding than sitting through lectures
  • 92.9% took greater personal responsibility for their own learning
  • 93.9% developed stronger critical thinking and problem-solving
  • 83.7% felt significantly more confident in their ability to learn difficult concepts
Peer feedback trained real-world collaboration
  • 89.3% found reviewing peer ideas stimulating and a richer learning experience
  • 89.3% reported a marked improvement in teamwork and communication
What the instructors said

Of the 13 instructors (of 22) who completed the survey, 100% said having students engage with new information on their own and collaboratively before class, then using class time for feedback instead of a lecture, was more productive and a better use of everyone's time. The study opens with the problem they all named: “students don’t come to class prepared” — and notes that most students do poorly under lecture-centred instruction because they struggle with rote learning.

Why it works: the practice cycle

Traditional instruction asks students to sit quietly while an instructor talks, leaving the hardest part — applying the knowledge — for homework, when the learner is isolated and frustrated. The pilot inverted that with a five-stage loop:

  1. Try it. The student tackles an open-ended challenge alone, focusing on effort and problem-solving rather than recall.
  2. See peer ideas. They read anonymous attempts from peers, sparking fresh perspectives on the same problem.
  3. Give and get feedback. They write constructive feedback and receive it, sharpening critical reasoning and self-correction.
  4. Synthesise. In the university pilot, small groups combined their ideas into a stronger solution.
  5. Guided mastery. The mentor or parent steps in — not to lecture, but to target the specific gaps the earlier stages revealed.
What this means for your child

Whether in a university lecture hall or at a homeschool kitchen table, the core finding holds: children learn far more from what they reason through themselves than from what they are told. ThinkSmith gives your child the structure, real-time coaching and feedback loop to become an independent, confident learner — supported by evidence from thousands of students.
