How children actually learn

The core insight: teaching others is the most powerful way to learn.

This isn't a new idea — researchers have studied it for decades. ThinkSmith is the first platform built entirely around it, at the scale of one child working from home. The 17.31% exam improvement wasn't produced by better teachers or harder content. The instructors, courses, and students were the same as previous years. The only variable was this method.

STAGE 1

Engage

Your child writes their own solution to a real academic challenge, in their own words — not multiple choice, not fill-in-the-blank.

STAGE 2

Evaluate — the key step

They read three peer attempts at the same challenge and write structured feedback to each. This is where the deep learning happens — evaluating others' reasoning sharpens their own.

This is the step most platforms skip entirely
STAGE 3

Reflect

They receive feedback on their own work and write a reflection on what changed in their thinking — the closest thing to a one-on-one teacher conference at this scale.

STAGE 4

See their Thinking Outcome

A synthesis across five competencies and a narrative of how their thinking developed that activity. They keep going if they want — the next activity is theirs to choose.

Safeguarding

What we share if a child may be at risk

ThinkSmith is an educational tool, not a crisis line or counselling service. If a child is in immediate danger, please call your local emergency services.
How our safeguards work

ThinkSmith runs on two layers of protection: the foundation provided by the AI model itself, and the layer we build on top for our own classroom.

Tier 1 — Foundation. Our AI coach is built on Claude, made by Anthropic. Anthropic's own safety systems apply to everything the coach produces, preventing harmful, illegal, or exploitative content at the source. This protection is always on and does not depend on us.

Tier 2 — ThinkSmith. The foundation layer cannot know what a good lesson looks like, or what Ms. Rivera is supposed to be. That is our responsibility, and it is where we concentrate our work.

  • Every message a child writes is screened for signs of distress before our coach replies. If content indicates self-harm or serious distress, the coach does not answer the question — see below.
  • Ms. Rivera has no memory of your child. She sees the challenge in front of her and, at most, the last few exchanges about that one piece of work. Nothing carries between challenges. She builds no profile, forms no impression over time, and cannot be gradually shaped by one child into something she is not. When a child submits the same work again, she reviews it fresh rather than judging them for asking twice.
  • She works to fixed teaching rules. She guides thinking rather than supplying answers — that is the whole point of ThinkSmith — and those rules are the same for every child, every time.
  • Every exchange is stored, and our team reviews them. Conversations between a child and the coach are retained and read by ThinkSmith team members as part of our ongoing quality and safety checks.

We are continuing to add to this layer. It is the part of the system we work on most.

If a child may be at risk
  1. Immediate support. The coach stops coaching and directs the child to a crisis service staffed by trained people — the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline in the United States, or Kids Help Phone in Canada.
  2. Human review. The alert is escalated to a ThinkSmith team member who has completed child-protection orientation.
  3. Reporting. Where a report to child-protection authorities is required by law, we notify the appropriate agency.
What information is shared

To allow an authority to assess immediate risk and contact a parent, we share only:

  • The learner's full name and grade
  • Their province or state
  • The parent or guardian's name and email address

We do not include a home address, date of birth, telephone number, location data or billing details in a safety report.

Household disclosures

If a child discloses a concern involving someone in their own household, we report to the authority without contacting the family first. A system that routed every disclosure straight back to a parent account could place a child at greater risk.

The research · parent version

How a 1,742-student university pilot proved that active thinking beats passive listening

When parents look for a learning approach for their children, they usually ask two questions: will this actually help my child learn better, and will it build the self-reliance they need for the future?

In 2008–2009, ThinkSmith founder Leslie Richards co-led a university pilot study involving 1,742 students across 22 courses in STEM, computer science and engineering at Ubon Ratchathani University.

The goal was simple: engage students in new knowledge before the lecture. Instead of sitting through passive hours of instruction, students used the ThinkSmith framework — active problem-solving, reviewing peer ideas, and receiving structured feedback ahead of the deep-dive discussion.

The findings parents care about
17.3%
higher final exam scores, across the 1,256 students where a direct comparison against traditional instruction was possible
44.8%
exam improvement in CAD & Engineering — dropouts and failures fell from 10 to 3
27.6%
higher final exam average in Calculus III, a retake course with a history of frequent failure
Students gained confidence and took ownership

Academic improvement only happens when a learner’s mindset changes first. In a post-study survey of 684 students:

  • 93.9% found ThinkSmith more rewarding than sitting through lectures
  • 92.9% took greater personal responsibility for their own learning
  • 93.9% developed stronger critical thinking and problem-solving
  • 83.7% felt significantly more confident in their ability to learn difficult concepts
Peer feedback trained real-world collaboration
  • 89.3% found reviewing peer ideas stimulating and a richer learning experience
  • 89.3% reported a marked improvement in teamwork and communication
Why it works: the practice cycle

Traditional instruction asks students to sit quietly while an instructor talks, leaving the hardest part — applying the knowledge — for homework, when the learner is isolated and frustrated. The pilot inverted that with a five-stage loop:

  1. Try it. The student tackles an open-ended challenge alone, focusing on effort and problem-solving rather than recall.
  2. See peer ideas. They read anonymous attempts from peers, sparking fresh perspectives on the same problem.
  3. Give and get feedback. They write constructive feedback and receive it, sharpening critical reasoning and self-correction.
  4. Synthesise. In the university pilot, small groups combined their ideas into a stronger solution.
  5. Guided mastery. The mentor or parent steps in — not to lecture, but to target the specific gaps the earlier stages revealed.
What this means for your child

Whether in a university lecture hall or at a homeschool kitchen table, the core finding holds: children learn far more from what they reason through themselves than from what they are told. ThinkSmith gives your child the structure, real-time coaching and feedback loop to become an independent, confident learner — supported by evidence from thousands of students.
